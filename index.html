The opportunity

Years of real work leave a record of how specialist work gets done.

AI systems learn from examples. Public information online shows little of how an architect handles a design change, how an accountant resolves a tricky query or how a logistics team recovers a missed delivery. Established businesses hold examples like these in their everyday records.

The most useful records tend to show a piece of work from start to finish: the request, the discussion, the decision and the outcome.

  • Project histories and revisions

    Files that show how a project changed, from first brief to final version.

  • Internal procedures and documentation

    Checklists, manuals and methods your team actually follows.

  • Operational communications

    Email and message threads where work was discussed and agreed.

  • Support tickets and problem resolution

    Issues raised, investigated and closed, with a note of what fixed them.

  • Estimates, job records and change requests

    Quotes, job sheets and variations, and how they compared with the final result.

  • Technical drawings and specifications

    Drawings, revisions and specifications produced in the course of the work.

What decides whether records are suitable

Having records is not the same as having records someone can license. Each of these is assessed case by case.

The records themselves
What they contain, and whether they show real work rather than finished outputs alone.
Their quality
How complete, consistent and well organised they are.
Retained history
How many years you have kept, and whether the steps of the work stay linked across your systems.
Ownership
Whether your business owns the records and can authorise their use, given client, contract and confidentiality terms.
Partner requirements
What a licensing partner is looking for at the time.

Many businesses will not have suitable records. If yours do not look like a fit, we will tell you plainly.

Relevant records

Examples by industry

These are illustrative record types, not case studies. Whether any of them can be licensed depends on who owns them, what agreements cover them and what a partner needs.

Architecture practices

Practices often keep many years of project files that show how designs develop, change and get resolved.

Fictional example

RFI-0117 · Stair core landing width

Raised 03.03.19 · Answered 06.03.19 · Rev C issued 14.03.19

May be relevant

  • Drawing issue registers and revision histories
  • Design change records and the reasons for them
  • Requests for information and the responses
  • Specifications as they developed through each stage
  • Internal design review notes and checklists

Needs checking first

  • Rights in drawings and designs under client appointments
  • Material produced by other consultants
  • Client correspondence covered by confidentiality terms
  • Personal data about clients, staff and contacts

Accounting firms

Firms build detailed internal methods for preparing, reviewing and checking work. Client records are a separate matter.

Fictional example

Review checklist v14 · Year-end close

38 steps · Updated after 2022 quality review

May be relevant

  • Internal procedures, review checklists and work programmes
  • Records of how technical queries were raised and resolved internally
  • Training material and guidance written by the firm
  • Templates and standard workpapers developed in-house
  • Quality review findings and how they were addressed

Needs checking first

  • Client files, returns and financial records, which are confidential and are not assumed to be eligible
  • Engagement letter and professional body obligations
  • Personal data about clients and their staff
  • Records held under regulatory retention rules

Construction contractors

Contractors record how jobs were priced, changed, built and finished, often over many years of projects.

Fictional example

Variation 042 · Additional drainage run

Requested 11.05.21 · Priced 14.05.21 · Approved 19.05.21

May be relevant

  • Estimates, take-offs and tender breakdowns
  • Change requests, variations and how they were agreed
  • Site diaries, method statements and inspection records
  • Snagging and defect logs, from report to fix
  • Programme revisions and the reasons for them

Needs checking first

  • Records owned by the client or main contractor under contract
  • Subcontractor and supplier information
  • Personal data in site records, photographs and timesheets
  • Pricing that others shared with you in confidence

Logistics operators

Operators handle large numbers of exceptions every week, and their records show how each one was dealt with.

Fictional example

Exception 88213 · Missed delivery slot

Logged 11:05 · Rebooked 14:20 · Delivered next day

May be relevant

  • Standard operating procedures and depot instructions
  • Exception and incident logs, with resolutions
  • Routing and scheduling decisions, and changes to them
  • Customer service tickets and claims handling
  • Failed delivery follow-up records

Needs checking first

  • Customer and shipper data covered by contract
  • Personal data about drivers, recipients and staff, including location data
  • Records held in a customer’s or partner’s system
  • Security-sensitive operational information

Other professional services

Engineering, surveying, consultancy and technical service firms keep records of how work was scoped, produced and checked.

Fictional example

Ticket SD-20431 · Connection drops after update

Priority 2 · Root cause found · Resolved in 3 h 40 min

May be relevant

  • Proposals and scopes, and how they changed
  • Internal methods, frameworks and quality reviews
  • Service desk tickets and their resolutions
  • Project close-out reports and lessons learned
  • Calculations, technical reports and checking records

Needs checking first

  • Deliverables and materials owned by your clients
  • Legally privileged or regulated material, which should be left out
  • Confidential client information under engagement terms
  • Personal data about client contacts and staff

Owner questions

You stay in control of your records

What to expect before anything is shared. The details of any licence depend on the agreement you sign, so read it carefully and take your own advice.

During an enquiry, we do not ask for

  • Files or samples
  • Passwords or access to your systems
  • Customer or client lists
  • Personal data about your staff or clients

Who will assess the opportunity?

We make an initial assessment of potential fit, based on what you tell us. If you agree to an introduction, the licensing partner carries out its own evaluation. We do not certify the value, quality or legal status of your records.

What information is needed initially?

Your company details and a description of the records you keep: what types, which systems they sit in and roughly how many years they cover. That is all.

Who decides whether to proceed?

You do. You decide whether we introduce you, what you share with a partner and whether to accept any licence. You can decline at any stage before an agreement is signed.

What uses would a licence permit?

Only the uses set out in the agreement. The scope, the recipients, the permitted uses, any exclusivity, the duration and the payment all depend on the actual agreement you reach with the partner.

What happens before records are transferred?

Before any records leave your control, a partner would normally agree confidentiality and evaluation terms with you, confirm which records are in scope and that you can authorise their use, and explain how personal and confidential information will be handled. The exact steps depend on the partner and on what you agree.

We cannot promise that an offer will be made, what records would be worth, or that every detail in them can be anonymised. Those depend on the records, the partner and the agreement.

FAQs

Questions owners ask

Short, plain answers. If yours is not here, ask us when you get in touch.

What does data licensing mean?

It means giving another organisation permission to use certain records, for set purposes, under a written agreement. The agreement says what is covered, who can use it, for what, for how long and what is paid.

What kinds of businesses might qualify?

Usually established businesses that have kept several years of well-organised records of specialist work, and that own those records. We are starting with architecture, accounting, professional services, construction and logistics. Suitability depends on the records, not only the sector.

Does our company need to be closing or for sale?

No. Trading businesses can enquire. If you are planning a sale, merger or closure, tell us, because it may affect timing and terms.

How much could our records be worth?

We cannot say without an assessment, and we will not guess. Value depends on the records, their quality and history, ownership, the preparation needed and what a partner requires. We cannot guarantee that an offer will be made.

Do we need to upload anything now?

No. Please do not send files. The first step is a short form or a conversation.

Can European companies participate?

Yes. We work with established businesses across Europe. Data protection law, including the GDPR, and contract terms apply to every case, so country-specific points are checked as part of the process.

Who handles preparation and licensing?

The licensing partner, working directly with you. Connexah Data Solutions is an introduction business. We do not buy, hold or prepare records, and we do not act for AI companies.

How does Connexah get paid?

If an introduction leads to a completed transaction, the licensing partner may pay us a referral fee. We explain that arrangement before we make any introduction, so you can take it into account.

Is our enquiry kept confidential?

We use your enquiry to reply to you and to assess potential fit. We do not pass your details to a licensing partner without your permission. Our privacy notice explains how we handle personal information.
